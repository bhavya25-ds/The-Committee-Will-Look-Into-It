## Chart 1

### Delhi Districts by Section 144 Order Count

**Observations:**
- The highest Sec 144 counts are in Shahdara (578), North-East (541), North (520), and East (508) districts — not Central Delhi, which sits near the bottom at just 28 orders.
- This is the opposite of what anyone would expect if Sec 144 was primarily being used to suppress protest near Parliament and Jantar Mantar.
- **North-East Delhi** saw severe communal violence during the Delhi riots of February 2020, and the anti-CAA protests that preceded them were heavily concentrated here. Sec 144 being imposed at 541 orders in North-East Delhi — while Central Delhi (Chandni Chowk, Daryaganj) sits at 28 — points toward its use as population control in a specific geography rather than protest suppression at the political centre.
- **Shahdara** is a high-density, mixed working-class district (Preet Vihar, Vivek Vihar, Dilshad Garden) whose 578-order count — the highest in Delhi — more likely reflects routine crowd and law-and-order management in a heavily commercialised, congested district. That Shahdara tops the chart through volume rather than political targeting is itself a finding: Sec 144 is normalised as a daily administrative instrument, not just an emergency one. It is worth noting, however, that Shahdara also contains Mustafabad and Seelampur, which were part of the anti-CAA protest geography — the two explanations are not mutually exclusive.
- **New Delhi District** — which covers Parliament Street, Connaught Place, and Jantar Mantar — records 383 orders. This is solidly in the top third of the chart, confirming that Sec 144 near the political centre is in fact being applied regularly, just not at the same volume as the denser peripheral districts.
- **Central District's** low count (28) stands on its own as a data point. It is the smallest geographic district in Delhi and covers Karol Bagh, Paharganj, and parts of New Delhi — areas with lower residential density and fewer large gatherings relative to East or North Delhi. The number should be read as-is.
- The **Metro** entry at 112 is a real Delhi Police operational district covering the DMRC network. 112 Sec 144 orders applied across metro corridors and stations is notable — it reflects either routine safety orders at sensitive stations or orders tied to specific events where metro access was restricted. It does not belong in the same category as geographic districts and is correctly at the bottom of the chart.
- **IGI (6), Railway (5), Special Commissioners (2), PHQ (2)** are operational units, not geographic districts — their low counts reflect targeted orders at specific choke points rather than area-wide imposition.

## Chart 2

### Top States by Internet Shutdowns (Sep 2024–Jul 2026)

**Observations:**
- J&K leads at 20, Manipur at 14, Odisha and Rajasthan tied at 12. This is the dataset's cumulative picture across September 2024 to July 2026.
- **J&K at 20** is a structural reality — the state has been under repeated internet restrictions since August 2019 and the abrogation of Article 370. Even within this dataset's window, the pattern continues: 20 shutdowns across roughly 22 months, extending what Access Now documented as one of the most sustained internet restriction regimes imposed by any democracy.
- **Manipur at 14** reflects the ongoing fallout of the ethnic violence between Meitei and Kuki-Zo communities that began May 3, 2023. The initial shutdown — imposed statewide on May 3, 2023 — ran through successive 5-day renewals. Broadband was partially restored in September 2023 after 142 days, mobile internet restrictions in affected districts continued well past that, running for over 200 days in total according to SFLC.in and Access Now. By the time this dataset begins (September 2024), Manipur was still recording repeated short-duration orders tied to ongoing violence.
- **Odisha at 12** is significant — it doesn't carry the same profile as J&K or Manipur. Odisha's shutdowns in this window are driven primarily by exam-related orders (Malkangiri district cluster) and communal-tension incidents (Bhadrak, Cuttack). Matching Rajasthan at 12 over this period marks it as a consistent, if lower-profile, practitioner of shutdowns.
- **Haryana at 7** — within this dataset's window (Sep 2024 onward), Haryana's shutdowns are tied to a combination of communal incidents and law-and-order situations in districts including Nuh, Faridabad, and parts of Gurugram. The well-documented Nuh communal violence shutdowns of July–August 2023 fall outside this dataset's scope.
- **Delhi at 4** — all four Delhi shutdowns are in July 2026, tied directly to the 'Chalo Sansad' march and its aftermath. The dataset records zero Delhi shutdowns in 2024 or 2025. On July 20, 2026, the Internet Freedom Foundation confirmed that mobile internet was suspended in parts of Central Delhi including the area around Jantar Mantar, coinciding with the CJP march. IFF's statement noted that no suspension order had been made public — a direct violation of the Supreme Court's Anuradha Bhasin ruling and the Telecommunications (Temporary Suspension of Services) Rules, 2024. Telecom companies confirmed receipt of the directive to The Wire; the issuing authority was not publicly disclosed. The Supreme Court subsequently directed the order to be published.

## Chart 3

### Preventive vs Reactive Shutdowns (63 vs 36)

**Observations:**
- **63 preventive, 36 reactive** — 64% of all shutdowns in the dataset are preventive. Total: 99 shutdowns across the dataset.
- Shutdowns are classified as **preventive** if no triggering event was documented prior to the order being issued, and as **reactive** if a specific incident — violence, a riot, a crisis — preceded it. The classification is based on documented sequence of events at the time of each order.
- A shutdown coded as **reactive** means something had already happened and the government cut communication to contain the fallout. A shutdown coded as **preventive** means the government cut communication in anticipation of unrest. No triggering event. No published order in many cases. Pre-emptive silencing.
- The July 20, 2026 Delhi shutdown is the clearest illustration: the CJP organised the 'Chalo Sansad' march from Jantar Mantar to Parliament on the opening day of the Monsoon Session. Permission for the march was refused. Section 163 BNSS prohibitory orders were imposed. Metro stations including Patel Chowk, Rajiv Chowk, and Janpath were closed. Mobile internet was then suspended in parts of Central Delhi, reportedly "as a precautionary measure," while the protest was still being cleared. IFF reported that no suspension order was published — confirmed by The Wire and The Week. That shutdown sits in the preventive column. The government cut internet to a peaceful march before any violence occurred.
- 63 preventive shutdowns across this dataset means this is not exceptional use of an emergency tool. It is routine governance.

## Chart 4

### Manipur Deep Dive (Nov 2024–Apr 2026)

**Observations:**
- The chart shows three clusters: **Nov 2024 (4 shutdowns)** and **Dec 2024 (2 shutdowns)**, dropping to near-zero through early 2025, a single shutdown in **June 2025**, a minor uptick in **Feb 2026 (2 shutdowns)**, then spiking to **5** in **April 2026**.
- The Nov–Dec 2024 cluster is the tail end of active ethnic violence escalation — Manipur imposed repeated shutdowns across Imphal East, Imphal West, Bishnupur, Thoubal, Kakching, Kangpokpi, and Churachandpur through November and December, all coded preventive. The dataset begins September 30, 2024; earlier shutdowns from that year are not captured here.
- The **June 2025 shutdown** (preventive, covering Imphal East, Imphal West, Thoubal, Bishnupur, Kakching) confirms the conflict had not resolved during the apparent quiet period.
- The near-zero readings from January–May 2025 and July 2025–January 2026 do not mean the conflict ended. This period more likely reflects gaps in dataset capture — the conflict remained active, and IFF and SFLC.in both document continued restrictions in 2025 — rather than a genuine absence of orders.
- The **April 2026 spike of 5** is the sharpest reading on this chart. Five separate orders were issued across Imphal West, Imphal East, Thoubal, Bishnupur, and Kakching, triggered by fresh violence in the region. The pattern of short-duration orders renewing repeatedly is identical to the original May 2023 mechanism.
- The ethnic violence between Meitei and Kuki-Zo communities that began May 3, 2023 had resulted in **258 confirmed deaths and over 60,000 displaced** as of November 22, 2024, according to official government figures (New Indian Express, November 22, 2024). Unofficial estimates are higher.

## Chart 5

### Year-wise State Breakdown (2024 / 2025 / 2026)

**Observations:**

**2024** *(dataset covers Sep 30–Dec 31, 2024 — approximately one quarter of the year)*:
- **Manipur** dominates at 6, Tripura, UP, and Haryana at 2 each. UP's 2 shutdowns are in Sambhal and Bahraich, both communal-tension triggered. Tripura's 2 are tied to law-and-order incidents in the period.

**2025** *(only complete year in the dataset)*:
- Dramatic geographic shift. **J&K** at 16, **Odisha** at 10, **Rajasthan** at 7. **Manipur** falls to just 1 (June 2025).
- This is the most geographically distributed year — shutdowns spread across 13 states including Punjab, Bihar, Maharashtra, and Ladakh.
- J&K's 16 in a single year continues the structural restriction pattern. Odisha at 10 in 2025 is significant — multiple district-level orders, exam-related and communal-tension driven.
- A March 2026 report by the #KeepItOn coalition found that India recorded 65 shutdowns in 2025, the highest of any democracy in the world. This dataset's 54 for 2025 is a subset of that count, depending on classification and source methodology.

**2026** *(dataset covers Jan 1–Jul 24, 2026 — approximately seven months)*:
- **Manipur** jumps back to 7 (April cluster), **Rajasthan, J&K, and Delhi** each at 4.
- Delhi's 4 shutdowns are entirely from July 2026 — the dataset records zero Delhi shutdowns in 2024 or 2025. This is a pure 2026 phenomenon.

The year-wise shift: *Manipur-dominated (late 2024) → nationally distributed (2025) → Manipur + protest-driven Delhi spike (2026)*.

## Chart 6

### Annotated Political Timeline (2024–2026)

**Observations:**
- **2024: 17 shutdowns** — but the dataset begins September 30, 2024, meaning this covers one quarter of the year, not a full year. It is not comparable to 2025 on its own.
- **2025: 54 shutdowns** — the peak, and the only complete year in the dataset. The 2025 count is driven by J&K (16) and Odisha (10). Neither has a single clean political explanation — this is diffuse suppression across many states, many triggers, no single event. That is actually more concerning than a spike, because it means the shutdown mechanism is deployed as a default response to any local tension. The #KeepItOn coalition's independently documented figure for India in 2025 is 65 — the difference from this dataset's 54 reflects sourcing and classification differences.
- **2026: 28 shutdowns** — dataset runs to July 24, 2026. This is a 7-month count. Annualised, 2026 is on pace for approximately 48 shutdowns — below 2025's 54 but not a meaningful decline given the partial-year comparison.
- The CJP 'Chalo Sansad' march of July 20, 2026 — the direct trigger for Delhi's 4 shutdowns in this dataset — occurred on the opening day of the Monsoon Session of Parliament, with students demanding the resignation of Union Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan following the NEET paper leak controversy. Permission for the march was refused. Section 163 BNSS prohibitory orders were imposed. Lathi charges and tear gas were used. Mobile internet was suspended in Central Delhi. No order was published. The Supreme Court subsequently directed it to be published.
- The chart's shape — low, spike, apparent decline — can create the impression that suppression peaked in 2025 and is receding. It is not. 2024 covers 3 months of data, 2026 covers 7 months. The only complete year is 2025. Year-on-year comparison is only valid with this asymmetry explicitly held in mind.

## Note on dataset scope

The dataset begins September 30, 2024 and runs through July 24, 2026. The 2024 count (17) covers Q4 2024 only. The 2026 count (28) covers January through July 24, 2026 only. The only complete calendar year in the dataset is 2025 (54 shutdowns). Any year-on-year framing must account for this. All counts, classifications, and district-level attributions in this document are derived from the dataset unless otherwise cited.
