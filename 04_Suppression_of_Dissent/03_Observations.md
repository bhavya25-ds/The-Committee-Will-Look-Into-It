## Chart 1 

### Delhi Districts by Section 144 Order Count

**Observations:**
- The highest Sec 144 counts are in Shahdara (578), North-East (541), North (520), and East (508) districts — not Central Delhi, which sits near the bottom at just 28 orders.
- This is the opposite of what anyone would expect if Sec 144 was primarily being used to suppress protest near Parliament and Jantar Mantar.
- **North-East Delhi** is a predominantly `working-class`, `Muslim-majority area`. This district saw severe communal violence during the Delhi riots of February 2020, and the anti-CAA protests that preceded them were heavily concentrated here. Sec 144 being imposed at 541 orders in North-East Delhi — while Central Delhi (Chandni Chowk, Daryaganj) sits at 28 — points toward its use as `population control in a specific community` rather than protest suppression at the political centre.
- **Shahdara** is a high-density, mixed working-class district (Preet Vihar, Vivek Vihar, Dilshad Garden) — not Muslim-majority. Its 578-order count, the highest in Delhi, more likely reflects `routine crowd and law-and-order management` in a heavily commercialised, congested district. That Shahdara tops the chart through volume rather than political targeting is itself a finding: Sec 144 is normalised as a daily administrative instrument, not just an emergency one.
- **New Delhi District** — which covers Parliament Street, Connaught Place, and Jantar Mantar — records 383 orders. This is solidly in the top third of the chart, suggesting Sec 144 near the political centre is in fact being applied regularly, just not at the same volume as the peripheral, denser districts.
- Central District's low count (28) is also explained by the fact that blanket Sec 144 there is typically imposed through emergency orders that may not appear in this dataset's count methodology.
- The Metro, IGI, Railway, and PHQ entries at the bottom (112, 6, 5, 2) are operational units, not geographic districts — their low counts reflect that Sec 144 is applied to specific choke points rather than transit infrastructure broadly.

## Chart 2

### Top States by Internet Shutdowns (Sep 2024–Jul 2026)

**Observations:**
- J&K leads at 20, Manipur at 14, Odisha and Rajasthan tied at 12. This is your entire dataset's cumulative picture across the period September 2024 to July 2026.
- J&K at the top is a structural reality — the state has been under repeated internet restrictions since 2019 and the abrogation of Article 370. Even within this dataset's window, the pattern hasn't broken: 20 shutdowns across roughly 22 months, continuing what is documented as one of the most sustained internet restriction regimes imposed by any democracy.
- Manipur at **14** reflects the tail end of the `2023 ethnic violence between Meitei and Kuki-Zo communities`. The first shutdown started May 3, 2023, lasted **over 200 days**, and by late 2024 the state was still recording repeated short-duration orders triggered by ongoing violence.
- Odisha at 12 is the surprise. Odisha doesn't have the same profile as J&K or Manipur, but matching Rajasthan at 12 shutdowns over this period is significant. Odisha's shutdowns in this window are primarily `exam-related` (Malkangiri cluster, Dec 2025) and `communal tension-triggered` (Bhadrak, 2024 and 2025).
- Delhi appearing at 4 in this chart is directly tied to `protest activity in July 2026`, including the `July 20 'Chalo Sansad' shutdown` where mobile internet was cut in parts of Central Delhi. The Internet Freedom Foundation reported that **no suspension order was published by the Ministry of Home Affairs, the DoT, Delhi government, or Delhi Police — a direct violation of the Anuradha Bhasin Supreme Court ruling and the 2024 Telecom Suspension Rules**. All four Delhi shutdowns are in July 2026 — the dataset records none from Delhi before this.

## Chart 3

### Preventive vs Reactive Shutdowns (63 vs 36)

**Observations:**
- **63** preventive, **36 reactive** — 64% of all shutdowns in the dataset are preventive. Total: 99 shutdowns across the dataset.
- Classification is based on whether a triggering event was documented before the shutdown order was issued. A shutdown coded as **reactive** means something had already happened — violence, a riot, a crisis — and the government cut communication to contain the fallout. A shutdown coded as **preventive** means the government cut communication in anticipation of protest or unrest. **No triggering event. No published order in many cases**. Just **pre-emptive silencing**.
- In July 2026 this played out explicitly: on July 20, the CJP organised the 'Chalo Sansad' march from Jantar Mantar to Parliament. Mobile internet was suspended in parts of Central Delhi. The Internet Freedom Foundation reported that **no suspension order was published** — a direct violation of the Anuradha Bhasin Supreme Court ruling and the 2024 Telecom Suspension Rules. That shutdown is in the preventive column. The government cut internet to a peaceful march before anything happened.
- 63 preventive shutdowns across this dataset means this isn't exceptional use of an emergency tool. It's **routine governance**.

## Chart 4 

### Manipur Deep Dive (Nov 2024 – Apr 2026)

**Observations:**
- The chart shows three clusters: **Nov 2024 (4 shutdowns) and Dec 2024 (2 shutdowns)** dropping to near-zero through early 2025, a single shutdown in **June 2025**, a minor uptick in **Feb 2026 (2 shutdowns)**, then spiking to **5** in **April 2026**.
- The Nov–Dec 2024 cluster is the tail of active ethnic violence escalation — Manipur imposed repeated shutdowns across Imphal East, Imphal West, Bishnupur, Thoubal, Kakching, Kangpokpi, and Churachandpur through November and December, all coded preventive. The dataset begins September 30, 2024; the earlier September wave is not captured here.
- The **June 2025 shutdown** (Imphal East, Imphal West, Thoubal, Bishnupur, Kakching — preventive) indicates the conflict had not resolved during the apparent quiet period. The near-zero readings from Jan–May 2025 and Jul–Jan 2026 don't mean the conflict ended — shutdown orders either weren't issued formally or weren't captured in the dataset for those months.
- The **April 2026 spike of 5** is the sharpest on this chart. Five separate orders were issued between April 7–16, 2026, across the same districts — Imphal West, Imphal East, Thoubal, Bishnupur, Kakching — triggered by fresh violence in the region. The April 7 order is coded reactive; the subsequent four are preventive. The pattern of short-duration framing that historically extended is identical to the `original 2023 orders`.
- The violence in Manipur between the Meitei and Kuki-Zo communities has been ongoing since May 2023 with **258+ recorded deaths** as of late 2024.

## Chart 5

### Year-wise State Breakdown (2024 / 2025 / 2026)

**Observations:**

**2024** *(dataset covers Oct–Dec 2024 only)*:
- **Manipur** dominates at 6, Haryana, UP, and Tripura at 2 each. Haryana's 2024 shutdowns are tied to `farmer protest activity` — the February 2024 farmer march toward Delhi and the Shambhu border closure generated repeated law-and-order responses. UP's 2 shutdowns are in Sambhal and Bahraich, both communal-tension triggered.

**2025**:
- Dramatic shift. **J&K** explodes to *16*, **Odisha** jumps to *10*, **Rajasthan** to *7*. **Manipur** falls to just *1* (June 2025).
- This is the most geographically distributed year in the data — shutdowns spread across 13 states including Punjab, Bihar, Maharashtra, and Ladakh.
- J&K's 16 in a single year is the continuation of the structural restriction pattern. Odisha at 10 in 2025 is significant — driven primarily by the Malkangiri cluster (7 consecutive days in December 2025, exam-related) and the Bhadrak and Cuttack incidents.

**2026** *(dataset covers Jan–Jul 2026)*:
- Manipur jumps back to 7 (April cluster), Rajasthan, J&K, and Delhi each at 4.
- **Delhi's 4 shutdowns are entirely from July 2026** — the dataset records zero Delhi shutdowns in 2024 or 2025. This is a pure 2026 phenomenon, the direct data footprint of the NEET controversy and CJP protest activity.

`The year-wise shift: Manipur-dominated (late 2024) → nationally distributed (2025) → Manipur + protest-driven Delhi spike (2026)`.

## Chart 6

### Annotated Political Timeline (2024–2026)

**Observations:**
- **2024: 17 shutdowns** — but the dataset only begins September 30, 2024, meaning this count covers October through December 2024 only. It is not a full-year figure. The NEET 2024 controversy broke in May–June 2024 — before this dataset begins — so the absence of NEET-linked shutdowns in the 2024 count is a dataset scope limitation, not evidence that the government contained the narrative. The 17 count is dominated by Manipur (6), Haryana (2), Tripura (2), and UP (2).
- **2025: 54 shutdowns** — the peak, and the only complete year in the dataset. This is a 3× jump from the partial 2024 figure. The 2025 spike is driven by J&K (16) and Odisha (10) — neither of which have a single clean political explanation. This is diffuse suppression: many states, many triggers, no single event. That's actually more concerning than a single spike, because it means the shutdown mechanism is being deployed across the country as a default response to any local tension.
- **2026: 28 shutdowns** — but the dataset runs only to July 24, 2026, meaning this is a 7-month count, not a full year. Annualised, 2026 is on pace for approximately 48 shutdowns — below 2025's 54 but significantly above the partial-year 2024 figure. The NEET 2026 exam was held May 3 for 2.27 million aspirants and cancelled May 12 after revelations of paper leaks and NTA insider arrests. The CJP protest movement formally began June 28. The 'Chalo Sansad' march was July 20, with the Delhi internet shutdowns following on July 20–24. Those 4 Delhi shutdowns are captured in this dataset's 28.

## Note on dataset scope

The shape of Chart 6 — low, spike, decline — can create the impression that suppression peaked in 2025 and is coming down. It isn't. 2024 covers only 3 months of data, and 2026 covers only 7 months. The only complete year is 2025. Any year-on-year comparison must account for this asymmetry.
